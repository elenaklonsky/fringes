I.

Instruments of Abundance

Nearly all the work of being alive is done by molecular machines — proteins.

These tiny atomic factories are constantly working away to create a whole planet worth of diverse, complex life.

They manufacture substances; they store information; they do something about it.

They even bounce, wiggle, and walk around.

They are responsible for assembling everything from the first living being to an elephant to a conscious mind to an immune system and a redwood tree.

It’s as if nature couldn’t help but tinker.

All of that from just 20 standard amino acids.

Take this small alphabet, and start with a few simple local interactions, and you can generate an astronomically large space of possible proteins with extraordinary capabilities we take for granted every day.

And yet biology has explored almost none of it.

If you were to count the total possibilities of arranging them into a typical length protein it would be circa 2×10390

That total figure is even larger than the number of atoms in the observable universe.

And that would count only average-length proteins. Imagine that for a second.

The possibilities sit on a scale that extends far beyond the names of numbers we have in the dictionary.

Not for lack of trying — all 30 million species co-inhabiting Earth have been running millions of experiments every hour, in some form, for around four billion years, and yet the entire history of life turns out to be rather short when the search space starts with 390 zeroes.

There has not been enough time to try more than a tiny fraction of what could be made from those twenty-odd parts.

Even then, evolution is a one-way, path-dependent search.

It cannot step outside the life already here, try everypossible version, then choose the best one.

All of these exceptional capabilities, discovered by contingency over deep time.

We are only beginning to ask

what else might be possible?

what else might be possible?

II.

The Fringes of Reason

Ankit Singhal has a habit of taking complicated things apart into discrete, logical pieces, then looking for the most elegant way to explain how those pieces produce the system above them.

By the time he left Basel for Columbia, he had already spent years doing this in labs back home. Classical philosophy might have seemed like a strange next step, but Ankit describes it in almost the same terms:

What interests him is the structure beneath the surface.

Whether it was quantum mechanics, biology, or philosophy, complexity became easier to reason about once he had the instruments to find the essential parts of a system, and the logical rules to formalise how they interact, adapt, and change.

Alter the conditions, and a whole realm of counterfactual arrangements comes into view.

The world in front of us, after all,

is only one possible configuration of matter among many.

He was searching for the logical way to start with one reality and build entirely new ones.

As it turned out, one of the more important arrangements of matter in his quest would be a very ordinary one: he was assigned to share a room with Connor Lee.

Connor had grown up in Spokane, where almost nobody around him was interested in the things he cared about or obsessed over.

He might have spent those years building in near isolation if a teacher hadn’t noticed what he could do with his hands and refused to let that talent go to waste.

It didn’t.

Connor went on to win national competitions, then moved across the country to attend Columbia.

He was the kind of person who could build a robot for thirty thousand dollars, then sell it back to the engineer who’d told him it couldn’t be built for less than three hundred thousand.

Ankit and Connor had arrived in the same place by very different routes.

But they recognized in each other a refusal to treat engineering as an exercise in improving what already existed.

So when the two of them encountered an effort to genetically engineer trees to pull more carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, their shared question was unsurprising.

Why start with the tree?

It did collect carbon, sure.

But that was only one small part of an elaborate arrangement of roots, leaves, metabolism, reproduction, defence and several billion years of evolutionary baggage.

If carbon was the thing you wanted, there was no obvious reason to take the rest along with it.

The counterfactual answer became Anthrogen.

They began with cyanobacteria:

a simple biological chassis already primed to pull carbon dioxide from the air and turn it into the enzymes that keep a cell running, without much else getting in the way.

Together, they engineered a strain that was more photosynthetic than anything that had evolved on Earth.

But that still wasn’t satisfying.

The deeper they went, the more the cell began to look like a component rather than the product.

The carbon had to be turned into something—fuels, materials, polymers—and more of the chemistry they cared about might be separated from the organism altogether and rebuilt outside it, as a cascade of enzymes converting one molecule into the next.

Then the proteins themselves had to be redesigned from the bottom up.

At every layer, they kept coming back to the same question: if you had perfect data and a perfect model of the underlying physics, would you build biological systems the way we build them today?

At every layer, their suspicion was the same: probably not.

So they moved down another level, with enzymes becoming the object of interest.

An enzyme cascade was a sequence of protein operations: in principle, they could specify the outcome they wanted and compose the chemical steps needed to reach it.

Proteins offered a way to test that idea. They were the primitive physical object that could also act like an instruction.

If they could be designed and composed like computational building blocks, you could build biological systems with complex functions from the bottom up, rather than tweaking what evolution had already produced.

The challenge

was navigating the enormous space of what might be possible.

Every existing model was learning from the same source material: the catalogue of proteins evolution had already produced.

Ankit and Connor were interested in the much larger space beyond it.

So they built the tools they needed themselves: a model designed to reason about proteins from their underlying physical structure, and a lab capable of printing billions of unfamiliar proteins and testing them at scale.

Together, the two formed a closed loop between prediction and experiment, allowing Anthrogen to venture into regions of protein space natural selection had never reached.

Reason.

Print.

Measure.

Test.

Repeat.

Bit by bit,

Anthrogen had begun to make an impossible search space navigable.

Until proteins became designable objects:

functions could be specified, structures built and tested, and successful parts composed into larger biological systems.

A biological factory that can change its chemistry on command, producing different fuels, materials or polymers from the same underlying system.

A sentinel protein that can detect contaminants in the environment and adapt conditionally to never-before-seen threats.

A space shuttle protein that can carry therapeutics, navigating the body to a precise location to release a payload into a specific cell.

Networks of molecular machines that can continuously sense, compute and respond to the world around them.

From the beginning, abundance has been the accumulated result of deep time: a small set of molecular parts repeatedly rearranged through chance, selection and circumstance into the diversity of life.

New possibilities coming from ordinary contingencies, two people randomly assigned to share a room.

By the end, that abundance will transform from chance collisions into a self reinforcing design space.

Life, participating in engineering its own possibilities.
